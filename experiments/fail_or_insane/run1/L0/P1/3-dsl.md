# Conceptualization: Daily Workflow Design for Small Commercial Kitchens

## Domain Specific Language — Pass 1

## Natural Vocabulary and Concepts

---

# I. The Central Question

**What concepts and vocabulary exist naturally in designing the daily workflow of a small commercial kitchen?**

This document extracts the inherent language—the terms, concepts, and semantic relationships—that emerge organically from practitioners working in this domain. This is not imposed terminology but observed language: the words cooks, chefs, and kitchen staff use to describe, coordinate, and reason about their work.

---

# II. Foundational Concepts

## The Primal Vocabulary

These terms represent the most fundamental concepts—the atoms from which kitchen language is composed.

### The Station Concept

**Station** (n): A bounded workspace within the kitchen where specific types of work occur, typically organized around particular cooking methods or menu categories.

A station has:
- **Fixed equipment** — the tools and cooking apparatus that define what can be done there
- **Associated prep** — the preparation work that feeds the station during service
- **Designated ownership** — typically one cook responsible during service
- **Clear boundaries** — defined by what enters and exits, physically and operationally

*Natural language usage*: "The grill station is ready." "You're covering the sauté station tonight." "That prep belongs to the cold station."

**Line** (n): The arrangement of cooking stations in a sequence facing the service area, where active cooking and assembly occur during service.

*Natural language usage*: "I'm on the line." "Call the line when you need something." "Two tickets on the rail."

**The Pass** (n): The elevated counter or area where finished plates are assembled, checked, and handed to servers; the boundary between kitchen and service.

*Natural language usage*: "Plates up on the pass." "Expo, I need eyes on table twelve." "Hold it at the pass."

### The Order Concept

**Ticket** (n): The physical or digital record of a customer's order, containing items, modifications, and timing information.

A ticket carries:
- **Items** — what is being ordered
- **Modifications** — changes to standard preparation
- **Priority** — typically implied by time on the rail
- **Destination** — table or seat assignment
- **Timestamps** — when received, when fired, when completed

*Natural language usage*: "I need a ticket." "Two tickets, one Mod." "Ticket up, fire when ready."

**The Rail** (n): The metal rack where order tickets are held in view of the line cooks, typically ordered by time received.

*Natural language usage*: "Check the rail." "Rail is clear." "New tickets on the rail."

**Fire** (v): To begin cooking an item or order.

*Natural language usage*: "Fire the apps." "Fire one salmon, two steaks." "I'm fired on table seven."

**All Day** (n): The total quantity of a specific item currently in production or needed across all active tickets.

*Natural language usage*: "All day, four salmon." "What's my all day on fries?" "All day is eight covers."

### The Person Concept

**Expo** (n): The expeditor—the person who coordinates the pass, checks finished plates, manages ticket timing, and orchestrates handoffs to service.

The expo holds:
- **Visibility** — view of all tickets and all stations
- **Authority** — priority-setting and timing control
- **Quality judgment** — final check before service

*Natural language usage*: "Expo needs this." "Check with expo." "Expo, I'm plating."

**Line Cook** (n): A cook assigned to a specific station on the line during service, responsible for executing that station's items correctly and on time.

*Natural language usage*: "The line cook on grill has it." "Who's on sauté?"

**Sous** (n): The sous chef—the second-in-command who manages the line during service, handles problems, and supports the head chef.

*Natural language usage*: "Call sous." "Sous is handling it." "Check with sous on specials."

### The State Concept

**Weeds** / **In the Weeds** (n): The state of being behind, overwhelmed, or unable to keep up with current demand.

*Natural language usage*: "I'm in the weeds." "We're deep in the weeds." "Get me out of the weeds."

**86'd** (adj): Out of a menu item; unavailable for service.

*Natural language usage*: "We're 86'd on the ribeye." "Don't sell any more halibut, we're 86."

**On the Fly** (adv): Immediately, with minimal preparation, as an emergency response.

*Natural language usage*: "I need that sauce on the fly." "Make a new batch on the fly."

**Called** (v): The act of announcing an order or need loudly so the team is aware.

*Natural language usage*: "Calling all day on salmon." "I need some love on table eight—called."

# III. Temporal Vocabulary

## The Language of Time

### The Rhythm Terms

**Mise en Place** (n): Everything in its place—the state of being fully prepared before service begins, with all ingredients measured, tools laid out, and station organized.

*Natural language usage*: "Mise is done." "Check your mise." "We need better mise before service."

**Drop** (v): To place something in the cooking equipment—to start its active cooking process.

*Natural language usage*: "Drop the fries." "I'm dropping the salmon now." "Drop timing is critical."

**Through** (adj): Complete; an order or item that has been finished and sent.

*Natural language usage*: "Table nine is through." "I'm through on my end." "Mark that ticket through."

**Lead Time** (n): The time required to prepare an item from start to finish, used for planning when to fire.

*Natural language usage*: "What's the lead time on the braise?" "The risotto has a long lead time."

**In the Window** (adj): Within acceptable timing; on schedule.

*Natural language usage*: "That ticket is in the window." "Get it in the window." "This is out of the window."

### The Timing Adjusters

**Hold** (v): To delay or pause an item, typically at the pass or in a holding environment.

*Natural language usage*: "Hold that plate." "Can you hold for two minutes?" "I need to hold the dessert."

**Run** (n/v): A coordinated push to complete multiple items or serve multiple plates together.

*Natural language usage*: "Let's run these out together." "We're running the desserts now." "Do a run on table six."

**Course** (n): A group of items served together as a sequence in a meal.

*Natural language usage*: "Fire the next course." "Hold the dessert until the table finishes appetizers." "This table is ready for their main course."

# IV. Spatial Vocabulary

## The Language of Place

### The Zone Terms

**Back of House (BOH)** (n): The kitchen and all areas not visible to customers—the production side of the operation.

*Natural language usage*: "This is a BOH problem." "The BOH team needs support."

**Front of House (FOH)** (n): The dining room, bar, and customer-facing areas; also the staff who work there.

*Natural language usage*: "FOH needs the dessert course." "The server is waiting on the pass."

**Hot Line** (n): The section of the line with cooking equipment—grills, ranges, fryers—where active heat cooking occurs.

*Natural language usage*: "Hot line is slammed." "Get on the hot line if you can."

**Cold Line** (n): The section of stations dealing with cold food—salads, cold appetizers, pastry, and plate assembly.

*Natural language usage*: "Cold line is ready." "I need support on cold."

### The Movement Terms

**Flow** (n): The natural movement of food through the kitchen; the pattern by which items progress from prep to service.

*Natural language usage*: "The flow is good tonight." "This layout disrupts the flow." "We need to improve flow."

**Cross** (v): For items or people to intersect in ways that cause congestion or contamination risk.

*Natural language usage*: "Watch the cross at the walk-in." "No cross between raw and ready."

**Handoff** (n): The transfer of responsibility or items from one person or station to another.

*Natural language usage*: "Clean handoff." "The handoff was messy." "Standardize the handoff."

**Drop Point** (n): A designated location where items are placed for the next person to pick up.

*Natural language usage*: "Use the drop point." "Pick up at the drop point."

# V. Resource Vocabulary

## The Language of Constraints

### The Equipment Terms

**Equipment** (n): The fixed and movable tools of production—ranges, ovens, refrigeration, prep surfaces.

*Natural language usage*: "What's the equipment status?" "We have limited equipment."

**Burner** (n): A single stovetop cooking zone; also used as a unit of capacity.

*Natural language usage*: "I need a burner." "Two burners are down." "What's on burners?"

**Real Estate** (n): Available workspace—counter space, shelf space, or station area.

*Natural language usage*: "I'm out of real estate." "Free up some real estate." "Prime real estate."

**Reach-In** (n): A smaller refrigerated unit accessible from the line; contrasted with walk-in.

*Natural language usage*: "Check the reach-in." "I need space in the reach-in."

**Walk-In** (n): A large walk-in refrigerator or freezer; the primary cold storage area.

*Natural language usage*: "Put it in the walk-in." "The walk-in is packed." "Walk-in check."

### The Supply Terms

**Par** (n): The standard quantity of an item that should be on hand at any given time.

*Natural language usage*: "What's your par on salmon?" "We're below par."

**Par Level** (n): The target inventory quantity for each item.

*Natural language usage*: "Set the par levels for prep." "Par is off."

**Pull** (v): To retrieve items from storage for use at the line or prep station.

*Natural language usage*: "Pull for the line." "Pull from walk-in." "I need to pull the specials."

**86** (v): To run out of an item; to remove an item from service.

*Natural language usage*: "We're 86'd on the special." "Don't 86 the dessert."

# VI. Transformation Vocabulary

## The Language of Cooking

### The Method Terms

**Grill** (v/n): To cook on a dry, high-heat surface; the station where this occurs.

*Natural language usage*: "Grill two ribeyes." "I'm on grill tonight."

**Sauté** (v/n): To cook quickly in a small amount of fat over high heat; the station where pan-cooking occurs.

*Natural language usage*: "Sauté the mushrooms." "Sauté is backed up."

**Braise** (v): To cook slowly in liquid, typically after initial searing.

*Natural language usage*: "Braise the short ribs." "The braise needs more time."

**Roast** (v): To cook in an oven with dry heat.

*Natural language usage*: "Roast the vegetables." "What's in the roast?"

**Deglaze** (v): To use liquid to dissolve the fond (browned bits) from a pan.

*Natural language usage*: "Deglaze with wine." "Get that fond deglazed."

### The Quality Terms

**Temperature** (n): The internal temperature of cooked items, used as the primary indicator of doneness.

*Natural language usage*: "What's the temp on that steak?" "Temp is right." "Check the temp."

**Doneness** (n): The degree of cooking, typically applied to proteins.

*Natural language usage*: "What's the doneness?" "Medium rare doneness."

**Bounce** (v): To test the readiness of bread or pastry by pressing lightly.

*Natural language usage*: "Bounce the bread." "It's not bouncing yet."

**Reduction** (n): A sauce concentrated by boiling to evaporate water.

*Natural language usage*: "Finish the reduction." "The reduction is tight."

### The Safety Terms

**Temperature Danger Zone** (n): The range between 40°F and 140°F where bacteria multiply rapidly.

*Natural language usage*: "Don't leave it in the danger zone." "Get it out of the danger zone."

**Cross-Contamination** (n): The transfer of bacteria from one surface or food to another.

*Natural language usage*: "Prevent cross-contamination." "No cross between raw and cooked."

**HACCP** (n): The food safety system based on hazard analysis and critical control points.

*Natural language usage*: "Follow HACCP protocols." "This is a HACCP issue."

# VII. Coordination Vocabulary

## The Language of Teamwork

### The Communication Terms

**Call** (v): To announce something loudly so the team hears—orders, needs, status.

*Natural language usage*: "Call the order." "I need to call for backup." "Calling the specials."

**Mark** (v): To indicate completion or acknowledgment on a ticket.

*Natural language usage*: "Mark it." "Marked and done." "Mark that ticket."

**Ticket Called** (phr): The moment an order is announced to the kitchen, starting the production clock.

*Natural language usage*: "Ticket called." "When was this called?" "Got the call."

**Eyes** (n): Attention; looking at or monitoring something.

*Natural language usage*: "I need eyes on table twelve." "Keep your eyes on that."

### The Status Terms

**Ready** (adj): Prepared and available; also, meeting quality standards.

*Natural language usage*: "I'm ready." "Is the prep ready?" "That doesn't look ready."

**Up** (adj/adv): Complete; finished; available for service.

*Natural language usage*: "The order is up." "Get it up." "What's up from you?"

**Down** (adj): Unavailable; broken; or completed.

*Natural language usage*: "The fryer is down." "Down on my end."

**Clear** (adj): Free of orders; also, empty.

*Natural language usage*: "The rail is clear." "Get clear." "Clear the pass."

### The Relationship Terms

**Cover** (v/n): To assume responsibility for a station or task; one plate of food.

*Natural language usage*: "Can you cover sauté?" "I need coverage." "We have forty covers tonight."

**Love** (n): Attention; assistance; care.

*Natural language usage*: "I need some love on table eight." "Give it some love." "No love for the fries."

**Bandit** (n): An order added outside the normal flow—rush orders, special requests.

*Natural language usage*: "Got a bandit coming in." "Don't forget the bandit."

**Death Row** (n): The collection of tickets that are overdue or at risk of being late.

*Natural language usage*: "Table three is on death row." "Clear death row first."

# VIII. Operational Vocabulary

## The Language of Execution

### The Prep Terms

**Batch** (n/v): To prepare a quantity of something in advance; also, that quantity itself.

*Natural language usage*: "Do a batch of hollandaise." "What's left in the batch?"

**Portion** (v/n): To divide into measured amounts; a measured amount.

*Natural language usage*: "Portion the chicken." "Each portion is six ounces."

**Label** (v/n): To identify with date, contents, and other information.

*Natural language usage*: "Label everything." "Check the labels."

**Date** (v/n): To mark with preparation date; the date itself.

*Natural language usage*: "Date it." "What's the date on this?"

### The Cleanup Terms

**Clean as You Go** (phr): The practice of cleaning during production rather than after.

*Natural language usage*: "Clean as you go." "Practice clean as you go."

**Break Down** (v): To disassemble and clean equipment and stations.

*Natural language usage*: "Break down the station." "Break down at end of shift."

**Set Up** (v): To prepare a station for use.

*Natural language usage*: "Set up for service." "Set up the line."

**Sanitize** (v): To clean to food-safety standards.

*Natural language usage*: "Sanitize the surface." "Sanitize between items."

### The Transition Terms

**Shift** (n/v): A work period; to change roles or positions.

*Natural language usage*: "Who's on the morning shift?" "I'm shifting to grill."

**Handoff** (n): The transfer of responsibility at shift change or station change.

*Natural language usage*: "Handoff report." "Standardize the handoff."

**Station Change** (n): The moment when responsibility for a station transfers, typically at shift change.

*Natural language usage*: "Station change at two." "Announce station changes."

# IX. Problem Vocabulary

## The Language of Issues

### The Error Terms

**Misticket** (n): An incorrectly written order ticket.

*Natural language usage*: "That's a misticket." "Fix the misticket."

**Fired Wrong** (phr): An item that was started incorrectly or at the wrong time.

*Natural language usage*: "That salmon was fired wrong." "Don't fire wrong."

**Comp** (v/n): To remove an item from a bill as compensation for a problem.

*Natural language usage*: "Comp the dessert." "Two comps tonight."

### The Crisis Terms

**Fire** (n): A serious problem requiring immediate attention.

*Natural language usage*: "We have a fire." "There's a fire on line one."

**Emergency** (n): A situation requiring immediate response beyond normal workflow.

*Natural language usage*: "Call emergency." "This is an emergency."

**Recall** (v): To retrieve a plate that has already gone to service.

*Natural language usage*: "Recall table four." "We've had a recall."

# XIII. DSL Summary

## The Language System

### Core Domains of Vocabulary

| Domain | Core Terms | Function |
|--------|------------|----------|
| **Space** | Station, Line, Pass, Zone, Flow, Handoff | Describe where work occurs and moves |
| **Time** | Fire, Drop, Through, Hold, Run, Lead Time | Describe when things happen |
| **Resources** | Burner, Real Estate, Reach-in, Walk-in, Par | Describe what is available and constrained |
| **Transformation** | Grill, Sauté, Braise, Temp, Doneness | Describe how materials change |
| **Coordination** | Call, Mark, Cover, Love, Bandit | Describe how people synchronize |
| **Status** | Ready, Up, Clear, Weeds, 86'd | Describe current system state |
| **Problems** | Misticket, Fired Wrong, Recall, Fire | Describe what has gone wrong |

### The Semantic Core

At the heart of this language are five primitive concepts:

1. **STATION** — A bounded place where specific work occurs
2. **TICKET** — A unit of work defined by an order
3. **FIRE** — The start of active cooking
4. **UP** — The completion and send of a plate
5. **CLEAR** — The state of having no pending work

### The Coordination Verbs

The language is built around these action verbs:

- **CALL** — To announce information
- **MARK** — To indicate status
- **PULL** — To retrieve from storage
- **DROP** — To begin cooking
- **HOLD** — To delay
- **RUN** — To execute a coordinated send
- **COVER** — To assume responsibility
- **86** — To remove from availability

# XIV. Closing Reflection

## What the Language Reveals

The natural vocabulary of kitchen workflow is a **tightly integrated semantic system** where:

- **Space is expressed through stations and zones** — locations are defined by function, not arbitrary coordinates
- **Time is expressed through actions** — time is not abstract duration but action sequence (fire, hold, run)
- **Status is expressed through conditions** — states are described by their normalcy or deviation (clear, in the weeds, 86'd)
- **Coordination is expressed through imperatives** — the language is fundamentally a language of requests, announcements, and responses

This vocabulary emerged from necessity. In a high-pressure, parallel, time-critical environment, language must be:

- **Instantly recognizable** — single words carry complete concepts
- **Direction-neutral** — the same term works for any food type
- **Action-oriented** — words describe doing, not just being
- **Status-explicit** — conditions are unambiguous

The DSL documented here provides the **conceptual vocabulary** from which more formal specifications can be constructed. It is the native tongue of the domain—learned through practice, not documentation, but no less precise for its organic origin.
